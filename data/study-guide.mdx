## Doctrine

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What statute is 10 U.S.C. § 3252 and what conduct does it target?</summary>

  The Defense Department supply-chain-risk authority (originally NDAA 2011 § 806). It allows the Secretary to designate a source as a 'supply chain risk' — defined as the risk that 'an adversary may sabotage, maliciously introduce unwanted function, or otherwise subvert' a national security system. Legislative history and DoD implementing instructions show the statute was aimed at foreign intelligence services, terrorists, and hostile actors, not domestic vendors.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What statute is 41 U.S.C. § 4713 and what does it provide that § 3252 does not?</summary>

  FASCSA — the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act. Compared with § 3252, FASCSA provides 30 days' notice to the target, an opportunity to respond, interagency review, and judicial review in the D.C. Circuit under 41 U.S.C. § 1327. § 3252 has none of those features.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What three findings must the Secretary make in writing under 10 U.S.C. § 3252(b) before designating a source a supply-chain risk?</summary>

  (1) Consultation with procurement or other relevant officials of the covered agency; (2) a written determination that exclusion is necessary to protect national security by reducing supply-chain risk AND that less intrusive measures are not reasonably available; (3) notice to the appropriate congressional committees explaining the risk and bases.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> Under DoW's implementing regulations at 48 C.F.R. § 239.7304(a), which office must provide the risk assessment supporting a § 3252 designation?</summary>

  The Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence. In Anthropic's case, the assessment came from the Under Secretary for Research and Engineering (Emil Michael) instead — likely a procedural violation.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is 5 U.S.C. § 705 and why did Anthropic invoke it?</summary>

  The APA provision authorizing a reviewing court (or, in some cases, the agency) to postpone the effective date of agency action pending judicial review. Anthropic sought a § 705 stay in addition to a TRO/PI. Judge Lin granted a § 705 stay of the Hegseth Directive and Supply Chain Designation.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the three-prong test for a First Amendment retaliation claim under Ninth Circuit law?</summary>

  Per Arizona Students' Ass'n v. Arizona Bd. of Regents, 824 F.3d 858 (9th Cir. 2016): (1) plaintiff engaged in constitutionally protected activity; (2) the defendant's actions would chill a person of ordinary firmness from continuing to engage in the protected activity; (3) the protected activity was a substantial motivating factor in the defendant's conduct. If plaintiff makes prima facie showing, burden shifts under Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250, to defendant to show it would have taken the action regardless.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> Why did Judge Lin find Anthropic's public statements were protected First Amendment activity?</summary>

  Anthropic and Amodei spoke publicly on the contracting impasse (January 2026 essay; February 26 statement) about AI safety in the military and surveillance context. This is 'speech on matters of public concern' which is 'at the heart of the First Amendment's protection' per Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443 (2011).

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> How did Judge Lin distinguish American Federation of Government Employees v. Trump, 167 F.4th 1247 (9th Cir. 2026), which the government invoked?</summary>

  In AFGE, the challenged executive order had no retaliatory language on its face and had a 'legitimate grounding in national security concerns'; stray hostile remarks in a separate talking-points sheet were not enough. In Anthropic, by contrast, the Challenged Actions on their face indicate retaliation, and the government identified no legitimate national security justification for the breadth of the measures.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the 'reputation plus' test as applied to government debarment, and what circuit articulated it?</summary>

  D.C. Circuit (Trifax Corp. v. District of Columbia, 314 F.3d 641 (D.C. Cir. 2003); Old Dominion Dairy Prods., Inc. v. Sec'y of Defense, 631 F.2d 953 (D.C. Cir. 1980)): a deprivation of protected liberty occurs when government action imposes a 'broad preclusion' that 'seriously affect[s], if not destroy[s]' a company's ability to obtain contracts in its chosen field. A complete prohibition on practicing the profession is not required.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> Under Mathews v. Eldridge, what three factors govern the procedural-due-process balance, and how did Judge Lin apply them?</summary>

  (1) The private interest affected; (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used and the value of additional safeguards; (3) the government's interest and the burdens of additional procedures. Lin found Anthropic's interests in reputation and federal contracting eligibility were significant; the absence of pre-deprivation notice created a serious risk of error; and the government showed no exigency to justify bypassing pre-deprivation process.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the Webster v. Doe carveout and why does it matter for the § 3252 review bar?</summary>

  Webster v. Doe, 486 U.S. 592 (1988): even broad statutory preclusion-of-review provisions are read not to preclude constitutional claims unless Congress clearly intends to do so. The § 3252 review bar does not mention constitutional claims — so Anthropic's First Amendment and due process challenges survive even if the statutory bar applied.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What does APA § 706(2) require a reviewing court to do, and which subsections did Anthropic invoke?</summary>

  Per 5 U.S.C. § 706(2), a court 'shall' hold unlawful and set aside agency action that is (A) arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law; (C) in excess of statutory jurisdiction, authority, or limitations, or short of statutory right; (D) without observance of procedure required by law. Anthropic invoked all three subsections.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the arbitrary-and-capricious standard from Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm?</summary>

  An agency action is arbitrary and capricious if the agency: (1) relied on factors Congress did not intend it to consider; (2) entirely failed to consider an important aspect of the problem; (3) offered an explanation that runs counter to the evidence; or (4) gave an explanation so implausible it could not be ascribed to a difference in view or to agency expertise. 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983).

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the Department of Commerce v. New York pretext doctrine and how did Lin apply it?</summary>

  Department of Commerce v. New York, 588 U.S. 752 (2019): even where an agency has statutory authority, courts must reject action if the stated rationale is a 'contrived' pretext for the actual motive; courts are 'not required to exhibit a naiveté from which ordinary citizens are free.' Lin applied this to the Supply Chain Designation, finding the timing (all supporting records dated March 2-3, 2026), the inconsistencies (Michael's same-day cordial emails to Amodei; Hegseth's DPA threat days earlier), and the post-hoc nature of the record showed the rationale was pretextual.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the FAR provision the Lawfare commentary cites limiting the use of debarment, and what does it say?</summary>

  Federal Acquisition Regulation 9.402(b): 'The serious nature of debarment and suspension requires that these sanctions be imposed only in the public interest for the Government's protection and not for purposes of punishment.' (48 C.F.R. § 9.402(b))

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> Which two pre-existing D.D.C. cases provide the closest factual analog cited in the legal commentary?</summary>

  Luokung Technology Corp. v. Department of Defense, No. 1:21-cv-00583 (D.D.C. 2021), and Xiaomi Corp. v. Department of Defense, No. 1:21-cv-00280 (D.D.C. 2021). Both involved Defense Department 'Communist Chinese military company' designations vacated or preliminarily enjoined as arbitrary and capricious where DoD had failed to provide a basis or opportunity to be heard.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the only other publicly known supply-chain-risk designation under FASCSA, and why does it matter for the Anthropic case?</summary>

  In September 2025, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence issued the first FASCSA order against Acronis AG, a Swiss cybersecurity firm with alleged Russian ties, limited to intelligence community contracts. It is the only public use of either § 3252 or FASCSA. No domestic company has previously been designated. The lack of historical precedent reinforces the major-questions argument and the conclusion that the authority was designed for foreign threats, not domestic contract disputes.

</details>


## The PI ruling

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">volatile</span> What did Judge Lin's March 26, 2026 preliminary injunction actually enjoin?</summary>

  (1) Implementation of the February 27 Presidential Directive by all Defendant Agencies; (2) Implementation of the Hegseth Directive and the Supply Chain Designation by DoW and Secretary Hegseth; (3) Issuance or maintenance of guidance, directives, or communications implementing those measures. The order also stays the effective date of the Hegseth Directive and Supply Chain Designation under 5 U.S.C. § 705. Bond set at $100. Seven-day administrative stay built in.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What three legal theories did Judge Lin find Anthropic likely to succeed on for purposes of the preliminary injunction?</summary>

  (1) First Amendment retaliation; (2) Fifth Amendment procedural due process; (3) APA — contrary to law, in excess of statutory authority, and arbitrary and capricious. The Court did not reach Anthropic's ultra vires challenge to the Presidential Directive.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What contemporaneous statements did Judge Lin cite as direct evidence that retaliation was a substantial motivating factor in the Challenged Actions?</summary>

  Hegseth's X post calling Anthropic's position 'arrogance,' 'sanctimonious rhetoric,' 'corporate virtue-signaling,' 'defective altruism,' 'Silicon Valley ideology'; Trump's Truth Social post calling Anthropic a 'RADICAL LEFT, WOKE COMPANY' and 'Leftwing nut jobs' who 'made a DISASTROUS MISTAKE trying to STRONG-ARM the Department of War'; the Michael Memo's express reliance on Anthropic's 'increasingly hostile manner through the press' as a basis for the supply-chain risk designation.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What evidence undermined the government's claim of national-security exigency justifying lack of pre-deprivation process?</summary>

  (1) Anthropic's Usage Policy restrictions had been in place for over a year and DoW had previously praised the company; (2) DoW's own six-month transition plan suggests no urgency; (3) U.S. forces used Claude in active operations in Iran within days of the ban; (4) Days before, Hegseth had threatened to invoke the Defense Production Act on the opposite premise — that Anthropic was essential to national security.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What is the operative content of Judge Lin's preliminary injunction with respect to what the government can still do?</summary>

  The Order 'restores the status quo' as of February 27, 2026. It does not require DoW to use Claude and does not prevent DoW from transitioning to other AI providers, so long as those actions are consistent with applicable regulations, statutes, and constitutional provisions. It only bars implementing or enforcing the three Challenged Actions.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> Why was the Hegseth Directive's contractor-blacklist language ultimately held ultra vires?</summary>

  At the March 24 oral argument, government counsel admitted he was unaware of any statute authorizing Secretary Hegseth to issue the prohibition on contractors doing commercial business with Anthropic, and agreed the statement had 'absolutely no legal effect at all.' That concession appears at Dkt. No. 128 at 8-9, 13-14.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> How does the existence of the parallel DPA threat help Anthropic's APA arbitrary-and-capricious case?</summary>

  The two positions are logically incompatible: invoking the DPA Title I requires a determination that the technology is essential to national defense; designating a supply-chain risk requires a determination that the technology is dangerous. The government cannot credibly hold both positions about the same vendor in the same week. The whiplash is direct evidence that the supply-chain framing is pretextual.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What was the bond amount the Court required, and why was it set so low?</summary>

  $100. The Court found the government had not made any showing of cost or damages from the injunction — the PI does not require the government to purchase Anthropic's products. Bond amount is discretionary under Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(c). Cited Johnson v. Couturier, 572 F.3d 1067 (9th Cir. 2009).

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> Why did Judge Lin decline to enjoin the Executive Office of the President even though she enjoined the rest of the Defendant Agencies?</summary>

  Because there was no record evidence that EOP had taken any specific action to carry out the Presidential Directive. EOP remains bound by the standard FRCP 65(d) language barring action 'in concert or participation with' the enjoined defendants.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> Where in the docket can the Michael Memo be found, and why is its authorship significant?</summary>

  Filed as part of Defendants' opposition (Dkt. 96-2 at 6-9). Authored by Under Secretary Emil Michael — who had led the contract negotiations with Anthropic. Significance: 48 C.F.R. § 239.7304(a) requires the risk assessment to come from the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence, not the negotiator; Michael's authorship is itself a procedural defect, and his expressly retaliatory framing of Anthropic's 'increasingly hostile manner through the press' is direct evidence of retaliation.

</details>

## Commentary

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">stable</span> What unique constitutional theory did Harold Koh and co-authors raise on Just Security that is not (yet) in Anthropic's complaint?</summary>

  Bill of Attainder. The Just Security piece argues the Supply Chain Designation meets both prongs of the modern attainder test: (1) it applies with specificity (singles out Anthropic by name); and (2) it imposes punishment without judicial process. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 9, 10 forbid bills of attainder at both federal and state levels.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">volatile</span> What is the major-questions-doctrine connection the Lawfare commentary draws?</summary>

  Earlier in 2026, the Supreme Court held (per a Roberts plurality) that IEEPA's grant of authority to 'regulate...importation' did not authorize the President to impose emergency tariffs. The Court found no national-security exception to the major questions doctrine and treated 'lack of historical precedent' as a 'telling indication' that the claimed authority exceeded the statute's reach. Lawfare argues the same logic applies to § 3252: the statute targets 'adversary' sabotage of the IT supply chain, not domestic AI vendors disagreeing about contractual restrictions; there is no historical precedent for this use of the authority.

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">volatile</span> What did OpenAI's parallel deal with the Pentagon, announced the day after Anthropic's designation, reveal that strengthens Anthropic's pretext argument?</summary>

  OpenAI announced a classified deployment contract with the Pentagon that included three substantive red lines closely tracking Anthropic's: no mass domestic surveillance, no autonomous weapons, and no high-stakes automated decisions. If those guardrails are genuinely comparable, the government is punishing Anthropic for the same kind of restrictions it tolerates in a competitor — a classic disparate-treatment pretext signal. (There is debate among commentators about whether OpenAI's restrictions are in fact as strong as Anthropic's.)

</details>

<details className="study-card">
  <summary><span className="badge">volatile</span> Which prominent legal scholars have written commentary on this case, and what are their core takeaways?</summary>

  Alan Z. Rozenshtein (U. Minnesota / Lawfare) — designation 'won't survive first contact with the legal system'; multiple independent paths to relief. Harold Hongju Koh (Yale, former State Dep't Legal Adviser) — Bill of Attainder theory; pattern of pretextual retaliation by the second Trump administration. Mark Jia — drew the Luokung/Xiaomi analogies. Dean Ball, Saif Khan, Charlie Bullock — analytical commentary on supply-chain risk doctrine. The Cato Institute and a coalition including FIRE/EFF have filed amicus briefs.

</details>
