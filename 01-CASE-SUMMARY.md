## 2. The dispute in 90 seconds

Anthropic's Usage Policy for Claude has, since 2023, forbidden two specific use cases regardless of customer: (a) mass surveillance of Americans and (b) lethal autonomous warfare. The Department of War (formerly Department of Defense, renamed by Exec. Order 14347, Sep. 5, 2025) had used "Claude Gov" under that policy since March 2025 and had vetted, certified, and contracted with Anthropic at the highest levels (Top Secret FCL, FedRAMP High / DoD IL 4-5, a $200M July 2025 CDAO contract, an August 2025 GSA government-wide deal).

In late 2025, DoW began insisting that Anthropic strip the two carve-outs and accept "any lawful use" language across all DoW contracts. Anthropic refused. After private negotiations, both sides went public — Amodei's January 2026 essay, Anthropic's Feb. 26 statement explaining the company's position.

On **February 24, 2026**, Hegseth gave Anthropic a deadline of 5:00 p.m. on February 27. On **February 27**, just after 3:47 p.m. ET, President Trump posted on Truth Social directing **every federal agency** to immediately cease use of Anthropic's technology. About an hour later, Secretary Hegseth posted on X (a) designating Anthropic a "Supply-Chain Risk to National Security" and (b) declaring that "no contractor, supplier, or partner that does business with the United States military may conduct any commercial activity with Anthropic." On **March 3**, Hegseth issued the formal § 3252 determination, communicated to Anthropic by letter dated March 3 and received March 4. A second letter the same day invoked **41 U.S.C. § 4713 (FASCSA)** — the basis for the parallel D.C. Cir. action.

Anthropic sued in both forums on March 9.

---

## 3. The three "Challenged Actions"

Judge Lin's opinion groups the government's actions into three legally distinct measures:

1. **Presidential Directive** (Trump, Feb. 27, 2026, Truth Social): Order to every federal agency to cease use of Anthropic technology; cites no statutory authority.
2. **Hegseth Directive** (Hegseth, Feb. 27, 2026, X): (a) directs DoW to designate Anthropic a supply-chain risk and (b) "Effective immediately, no contractor, supplier, or partner that does business with the United States military may conduct any commercial activity with Anthropic"; characterized as "final."
3. **Supply Chain Designation** (March 3, 2026, formal determination + letters citing 10 U.S.C. § 3252 *and* 41 U.S.C. § 4713; congressional notification letters dated same day).

A telling concession at oral argument: government counsel admitted he was unaware of any statute authorizing Hegseth's contractor-blacklist language and conceded it had "absolutely no legal effect at all." That concession is now part of the record.

## 4. Anthropic's claims

From the complaint:

- **Count I** — APA, contrary to law and arbitrary and capricious as to the 10 U.S.C. § 3252 Supply Chain Designation (Hegseth, DoW)
- **Count II** — APA challenges to the Hegseth Directive's contractor blacklist (in excess of statutory authority)
- **Count III** — Fifth Amendment Due Process (procedural)
- **Count IV** — First Amendment retaliation (and, in the alternative, viewpoint discrimination)
- **Count V** — Ultra vires challenge to the Presidential Directive (in excess of statutory and constitutional authority)

## 5. The PI Order (3/26/2026): What Judge Lin held

Judge Lin issued a 43-page opinion granting the PI on March 26, 2026. The injunction reaches all 18 named "Defendant Agencies" (DoW, Treasury, State, HHS, DHS, Energy, Commerce, VA, SSA, GSA, NRC, NEA, NASA, Federal Reserve, FHFA, SEC, OPM, plus EOP-related actors) and Secretary Hegseth, restoring the *status quo ante* February 27. It does not require the government to use Claude; it only bars enforcement of the three Challenged Actions. The Court imposed a $100 nominal bond and a seven-day administrative stay (expired April 2).

The Court found likelihood of success on **three** grounds (the ultra vires theory was not reached):

### 5.1 First Amendment retaliation

Applied the three-prong *Ariz. Students' Ass'n* test (9th Cir. 2016):

1. **Protected activity.** Anthropic's public discussion of the contracting impasse — Amodei's January 2026 essay; the February 26 statement — is "speech on matters of public concern," "at the heart of the First Amendment's protection" (*Snyder v. Phelps*).
2. **Chilling effect.** Government did not dispute; record shows the Challenged Actions threaten to cripple Anthropic and have already chilled discussion of AI safety (amici briefs).
3. **Nexus.** The Court found this prong satisfied "easily" — Trump and Hegseth's own contemporaneous statements identify "ideology," "rhetoric," "arrogance," "sanctimonious rhetoric," and "Silicon Valley ideology" as the reasons for the action; the Michael Memo expressly cites Anthropic's "hostile manner through the press" as the basis for the supply-chain risk designation.

Burden then shifted to defendants under *Hartman v. Moore* to show they would have taken the same action absent the protected speech. Defendants pointed to the contracting impasse — but Anthropic's restrictions had been in place since March 2025 and had never previously been treated as risky. The same restrictions only became a "national security threat" after Anthropic went public. The Court distinguished *AFGE v. Trump* (9th Cir. 2026), where retaliatory statements were extraneous to an otherwise legitimately grounded executive order; here, the retaliatory framing is on the face of the action.

The Court analyzed under the *Ariz. Students' Ass'n* test rather than *Pickering*; noted in a footnote that the result would be the same under *Pickering* because the government made no balancing-test argument.

### 5.2 Fifth Amendment procedural due process

Applied *Mathews v. Eldridge* (1976) balancing.

**Protected liberty interest:** Citing *Greene v. McElroy* (1959), *Trifax Corp. v. D.C.* (D.C. Cir. 2003), and *Old Dominion Dairy v. Sec'y of Def.* (D.C. Cir. 1980), the Court held that all three Challenged Actions implicate Anthropic's protected liberty interest in (i) federal contracting eligibility and (ii) reputation. The Court rejected the government's argument that a "complete prohibition" of the right to engage in a calling is required: under D.C. Circuit's "reputation plus" test, a "broad preclusion" that "seriously affect[s], if not destroy[s]" a company's ability to obtain contracts is sufficient.

**No pre-deprivation process and no shown exigency:** Anthropic received no notice or opportunity to respond. The Court rejected the government's exigency theory — the parties had been operating under the very terms in dispute for over a year, and the government's own six-month transition plan, plus its use of Claude in active operations in Iran within days of the ban, contradict any urgency claim. Compliance with § 3252's stripped-down procedures does not satisfy the *Mathews* baseline.

### 5.3 APA — § 3252 designation likely contrary to law, in excess of authority, and arbitrary and capricious

The Court found three independent APA problems with the Supply Chain Designation:

**(a) Outside statutory text.** Section 3252 authorizes designation of an "adversary" who may "sabotage, maliciously introduce unwanted function, or otherwise subvert" a covered system. The text targets covert acts or hacks. Anthropic's overt contractual position cannot reasonably be characterized as sabotage. Government counsel at argument was unable to identify any record evidence that Anthropic has technical capability to alter or stop Claude in DoW environments after deployment. Reading "supply chain risk" to reach a vendor's negotiating posture would "make [Section 3252's] restrictions on the Secretary's discretion meaningless."

**(b) Procedural defects.** Section 3252(b)(2)(B) requires a written determination that "less intrusive measures are not reasonably available." Hegseth's six identical letters to congressional committees did not discuss less intrusive measures at all, despite § 3252(b)(3)(B) requiring such a discussion. Government counsel conceded this at oral argument. Additionally, 48 C.F.R. § 239.7304(a) requires the risk assessment to come from the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence; here it came from Emil Michael, the Under Secretary for Research and Engineering, who had led the contract negotiations.

**(c) Arbitrary and capricious.** Following *Department of Commerce v. New York* (2019), Judge Lin found the stated rationale pretextual. The "story" the record tells does not match the explanation given: Michael's same-day cordial emails to Amodei about contract drafts ("I think we are very close here") cannot be squared with his contemporaneous characterization of Anthropic as an "unacceptable national security threat"; Hegseth's threat days earlier to invoke the Defense Production Act (which would treat Anthropic as *essential*) cannot be squared with the "supply chain risk" framing.

The Court also held the Hegseth Directive ultra vires — government counsel conceded no statutory authority for the contractor blacklist.

The Court did not reach Anthropic's ultra vires challenge to the Presidential Directive, having already found three independent merits problems.

## 6. The D.C. Circuit FASCSA case

The parallel D.C. Cir. petition (No. 26-1049) challenges the separate 41 U.S.C. § 4713 letter — FASCSA, not § 3252. Three things to understand:

1. **Why two letters?** FASCSA is procedurally more protective (30-day notice, interagency review, judicial review in D.C. Cir. under § 1327). Section 3252 has none of those features. DoW issued both letters the same day. Anthropic challenged each in its statutorily appropriate forum.
2. **D.C. Cir. denied Anthropic's emergency stay** in early April 2026 — meaning the FASCSA designation is in effect for parts of the relief Judge Lin's PI did not reach. Commentary (Volokh, Jones Walker, KTS Law) reads the stay denial as procedurally driven, not a merits signal.
3. **The panel's three ordered briefing issues** for the May 19 argument:
   - Jurisdiction under 41 U.S.C. § 1327 over "covered procurement actions" under § 4713
   - Whether the Determination/Notice constitutes or directs a "covered procurement action"
   - Whether Anthropic can affect the functioning of its AI models before or after delivery to DoW

That third question goes to the heart of the supply-chain-risk theory — the same factual question Judge Lin answered against the government (Anthropic has no such capability in deployed environments).

After the May 19 argument, the panel ordered supplemental briefs (due 6/4/2026), suggesting the panel is wrestling with at least one of the briefed issues.

## 7. What the commentary says

Across the legal-academic and practitioner commentary, the dominant view is that the government's substantive position is weak — but the procedural posture across two circuits creates real complexity. A non-exhaustive map:

- **Lawfare (Endrias & Rozenshtein, 3/2/2026)** — "Pentagon's Anthropic Designation Won't Survive First Contact with Legal System." Identifies multiple independent grounds for relief: statutory text, *Webster v. Doe* constitutional carveout, *Department of Commerce v. New York* pretext analysis, FAR 9.402(b) (debarment must be in public interest, not for punishment), and analogies to *Luokung Technology Corp. v. DoD* (D.D.C. 2021) and *Xiaomi Corp. v. DoD* (D.D.C. 2021).
- **Lawfare (Rozenshtein, separately)** — Argues the DPA threat earlier in the week was equally legally infirm; the statute has never been used to compel a company to produce a product it has deemed unsafe.
- **Just Security (Koh, Swartz, Gupta, Worthington, 3/6/2026)** — Raises a Bill of Attainder theory not in Anthropic's complaint: the designation singles out Anthropic by name and imposes punishment without judicial process, satisfying both prongs of the modern attainder test. (Koh later appeared in the case as pro hac counsel for the "Former Senior National Security Government Officials" amicus group.)
- **Volokh / Reason (4/8/2026)** — Coverage of the D.C. Cir. stay denial.
- **AEI (multiple pieces)** — Defends procedural posture but agrees the social-media-driven nature of the action creates First Amendment exposure.
- **Cato Institute** — Filed amicus in both cases supporting Anthropic.
- **Breaking Defense, CNBC, CNN, CBS, NPR, The Hill** — News coverage; note Pentagon CTO Emil Michael's post-PI statement that the order had "dozens of factual errors" and that the designation remains "in full force and effect."
- **Jones Walker AI Law Blog ("Two Courts, Two Postures")** — Practitioner explanation of why the D.C. Cir.'s stay denial does not undermine Judge Lin's PI; the cases address different statutes and different government actions.
- **Wiley ("ICYMI: Developments in Anthropic Challenges...")** — Procurement-bar perspective on FASCSA mechanics.

## 8. Issues to watch

**Near-term (next 30-90 days):**

- Supplemental briefs due June 4 in D.C. Cir. → decision likely summer 2026
- N.D. Cal. cross-MSJ briefing through July; hearing July 30, 2026
- If D.C. Cir. rules for Anthropic, 9th Cir. stay likely lifts and PI affirmance becomes more probable
- If D.C. Cir. rules for the government on jurisdiction (no § 1327 review of this kind of "covered procurement action"), Anthropic loses one forum but its § 3252 claims in N.D. Cal. remain
- Watch for whether the government files an emergency stay application at SCOTUS following any unfavorable 9th Cir. or D.C. Cir. ruling

**Doctrinal questions in play:**

- Reach of the *Webster v. Doe* clear-statement rule against preclusion of constitutional claims
- Application of *Dept. of Commerce v. New York* pretext analysis to social-media-driven agency decisions
- Whether a § 3252-style stripped-down procedure satisfies *Mathews* when applied to a domestic, non-adversary contractor
- Whether the major questions doctrine, after the SCOTUS IEEPA tariffs decision earlier in 2026, applies to supply-chain risk designations
- Bill of Attainder revival (Koh et al. theory) — not yet pleaded but available as a backstop
- First Amendment retaliation doctrine in the government-as-contractor context — *Pickering*/*Garcetti* vs. *NRA v. Vullo* (2024) framework

**Strategic questions:**

- Does OpenAI's parallel DoW deal with similar guardrails (announced 2/28/2026) further support Anthropic's pretext theory? OpenAI's "no mass surveillance, no autonomous weapons, no high-stakes automated decisions" tracks Anthropic's restrictions almost exactly
- Will Anthropic add a Bill of Attainder count on amendment?
- What happens at SCOTUS if/when this gets there?
